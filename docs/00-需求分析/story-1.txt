The Last Light on Gull Island

Every evening for forty-one years, Elias Morrow climbed the one hundred and twelve steps of the Gull Island lighthouse and lit the lamp. He did it in summer, when the sea lay flat and silver like a sheet of tin, and he did it in winter, when the wind threw salt against the glass so hard that it sounded like gravel. He never missed a night. Not once.

His granddaughter, Nora, came to live with him the spring she turned twelve. Her mother had taken a job in the city, the kind of job that kept her on trains and in hotels, and so Nora arrived on the supply boat with a single suitcase, a box of books, and a look on her face that said she had already decided to hate everything.

"There's no internet," she said on the first night, holding her phone up toward the ceiling as if the signal might be hiding in the rafters.

"There's the sea," Elias said. "It's older than the internet, and it has better stories."

Nora rolled her eyes so hard that Elias was afraid they might stay that way.

For the first few weeks she barely spoke. She read on the rocks below the lighthouse, wrapped in two sweaters, and she ate her dinner without looking up. But every evening, at exactly the same time, she heard her grandfather's slow steps on the iron staircase, and every evening she noticed the beam sweep across the water, white and steady, reaching out toward ships she could not see.

One night in May, curiosity won. She followed him up.

The lamp room was smaller than she had imagined, and warmer. The great lens sat in the middle like a giant glass beehive, its rings catching the last of the sunset and breaking it into a hundred tiny rainbows. Elias wiped each panel with a soft cloth, checked the oil, wound the old clockwork that turned the light, and finally struck a long match.

"Why do you still do it by hand?" Nora asked. "Couldn't a machine do all this?"

"A machine could," he said. "And one day a machine will. The government has already sent the letters." He nodded toward a drawer stuffed with envelopes. "Next autumn they'll put in an automatic lamp, and I'll be sent to live on the mainland like a retired horse."

"Does that make you sad?"

Elias watched the beam reach the horizon and come back. "A light doesn't care who lights it," he said at last. "But the people who need it should know that someone is awake. That's the part a machine can't do."
