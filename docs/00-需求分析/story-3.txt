"Grandpa?"

"It's nothing," he said, but his face was grey and his breath came short. "Just my heart reminding me how old it is. Give me a minute."

Nora looked up the spiral of the staircase, then out of the narrow window. Through the rain she could see a small light on the water, rising and falling. A boat. Too close to the reef.

"I'll do it," she said.

"Nora, the wind up there—"

"I know how. You taught me."

She climbed the last fifty steps two at a time. In the lamp room the glass shook in its frame, and the noise was so loud that she could not hear her own breathing. Her hands trembled as she checked the oil and wound the clockwork, exactly the way she had watched him do it a hundred times. The first match blew out. So did the second. She cupped the third against her chest, counted to three, and touched it to the wick.

The flame caught. The lens woke up. And the beam swung out across the black water, white and steady, as if nothing in the world could stop it.

She stood at the window and watched the small boat. For a long, terrible moment it kept heading straight for the reef. Then, slowly, it turned. It found the channel. It slipped past the rocks and disappeared into the safety of the harbour.

When she came down, her grandfather was still sitting on the step. He did not ask whether she had done it. He had seen the light on the wall.

"Someone was awake," he said quietly, and squeezed her hand.

The next morning the fisherman from the boat rowed out to the island with a basket of fresh bread and a jar of honey. He said he had lost his engine and his radio, and that he had been sure he would die. "Then the light came on," he said, "and I knew which way was home."

The automatic lamp was installed the following spring, just as the letters had promised. Elias moved to a small house in the harbour town, where he could see the lighthouse from his kitchen window. Nora moved there too. Her mother came home more often now, and sometimes all three of them sat on the sea wall in the evening to watch the beam come on by itself.

It was never quite the same. But every night, at exactly the time her grandfather used to climb the stairs, Nora looked out toward Gull Island and said, very softly, "I'm awake."

And somewhere out on the dark water, she liked to think, someone was glad.
